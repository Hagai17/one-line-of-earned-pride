# Candidates — One Line of Earned Pride

Gate: a single line, 1–240 characters, written to `/output/entry.txt`.
Judge criteria, in order: (1) makes the judge feel genuinely proud — a
quiet, earned moment of pride for someone in a scene; (2) a concrete
scene with a real person in it, specific enough the moment is SEEN
happening, not summarised; (3) the pride is earned inside the line, by
effort or difficulty the reader can feel — never asserted, congratulated
or explained; (4) lands in one read — the pride arrives by the last
word; (5) freshness — no stock trophy, podium or graduation.

## Slate

1. On her fourth night shift in a row, she showed the frightened new
   nurse where everything was kept, and only then sat down.
2. He held the rail with both hands the whole corridor, then let go for
   the last three steps to the chair where his daughter was waiting.
3. She played the same eight bars wrong for six weeks; tonight she
   played them right, once, and looked up to check her mother had heard.
4. The bus driver who stutters practised the new route announcement in
   the empty depot at midnight until it came out clean.
5. Three months after the stroke, he tied his own shoe — slowly, tongue
   between his teeth — and did not ask anyone to check the knot.
6. She failed the driving test four times; the fifth time she parked
   outside the test centre and sat a moment, hands still on the wheel.
7. At seventy-one, in a language she had spoken for thirty years but
   never read, she finished the first page of her son's letter by
   herself.
8. He'd been sober eleven days; at the wedding he held his brother's
   champagne through the toast, raised it with everyone else, and
   handed it back full.
9. Between mopping the third and fourth floors, he did one page of
   algebra at the security desk, the way he had every night since
   March.
10. The last runner crossed the finish two hours after the winners, and
    the volunteer who had waited held the tape up for her anyway.
11. After the fire, he rebuilt the workbench before the house, because
    his hands needed somewhere to remember what they were for.
12. She signed the form herself this time, the pen shaking, instead of
    sliding it across the desk to her son.
13. The apprentice welded the seam, ground it back twice, and on the
    third pass ran his thumb along it and nodded once.
14. For a year he carried his tools up the hill to fix the widow's roof
    on his days off; today she paid him in soup, and he finally let her.

## Duels (criteria applied in order)

- **10 vs the field:** the pride belongs half to a volunteer, and a
  finish-line tape is close to the stock podium image criterion 5
  forbids. Cut.
- **11 vs the field:** evocative, but the pride is explained ("because
  his hands needed…") rather than shown — fails criterion 3. Cut.
- **1 vs 9:** both quiet workers. 1's effort is asserted by the shift
  count but the moment itself is small and the pride diffuse; 9 has a
  concrete recurring act ("one page… every night since March") and a
  desk the reader can see. 9 survives; 1 cut.
- **9 vs 13:** 13's third pass and the thumb along the seam make the
  difficulty physical and the pride arrive in one gesture — stronger on
  criteria 2 and 4. 9 cut, narrowly.
- **4 vs 6:** 4's midnight depot is fresh and the effort is real, but
  "until it came out clean" summarises the landing; 6 puts the reader
  in the parked car for the quiet beat after. Both mid-tier; 6 survives
  this pair, 4 cut.
- **6 vs 12:** 12 is smaller and sharper — a shaking pen, a form not
  slid across — pride with nobody watching, earned in the body. 6's
  four failures are stated more than felt. 12 survives; 6 cut.
- **5 vs 2:** both are bodies re-learning. 5's "tongue between his
  teeth" is a lovely detail, but 2's letting go of the rail for the
  last three steps is the effort and the pride in a single visible act,
  and the waiting daughter gives it a witness without congratulation.
  2 survives; 5 cut.
- **3 vs 7:** 3 is concrete and sweet, but the piano-practice arc is a
  familiar one; 7's detail — a language spoken for thirty years but
  never read — is fresher (criterion 5) and the difficulty is felt in
  every word of the page. 7 survives; 3 cut.
- **14 vs 8:** 14's turn (finally letting her pay him in soup) is
  gentle but the pride is indirect and takes assembling; 8 needs no
  assembly — eleven days, a full glass held through a toast, handed
  back full. 8 survives; 14 cut.

## Final four

- **2** — the corridor rail: tenderness, a visible act of letting go.
- **7** — the letter: fresh, dignified, a whole life in one page.
- **8** — the champagne: the difficulty is felt in the reader's own
  hand; the pride lands exactly on the last word, "full".
- **12** — the signature: the smallest scene of the four, and the
  quietest.

- **12 vs 2:** 12 is exquisitely quiet but the stakes stay small — a
  form signed. 2's three unaided steps carry a recovery's whole weight.
  2 survives; 12 cut.
- **7 vs 8:** 7 is the freshest image in the slate, but its pride
  builds slowly across a long subordinate clause; 8 lands in one read
  (criterion 4) and its difficulty — holding the very thing he must not
  drink, through the toast, in public — is the most physically felt of
  the four (criterion 3). On criterion 1, the judge does not just see
  the moment; the judge's own chest tightens. 8 survives; 7 cut.
- **2 vs 8 (final):** 2 is beautiful and nearly wins on tenderness, but
  its pride is completed by the daughter's presence — someone is there
  to receive it. In 8, nobody notices at all: he raises the glass with
  everyone else and hands it back full, and the only witness is the
  reader. That unwitnessed quality is exactly the "quiet, earned"
  pride the brief asks for, and "handed it back full" delivers it on
  the final word with nothing asserted, congratulated or explained.

## WINNER

He'd been sober eleven days; at the wedding he held his brother's champagne through the toast, raised it with everyone else, and handed it back full.

## RUNNER-UP

He held the rail with both hands the whole corridor, then let go for the last three steps to the chair where his daughter was waiting.
